The Shape of Economics: Mapping the Transformation of a Discipline
Tom Harris
2026
Abstract

How is economics evolving as a discipline—are its research areas converging or fragmenting, and what characterizes those that grow? I place 50,000 research articles published in 24 leading economics journals and the NBER working paper series between 2000 and 2026 on a map in which papers on similar topics sit near one another, and group them from the bottom up into 46 research clusters. Roughly a fifth of publication share moved across fields, away from macroeconomics, finance, and microeconomic theory and toward applied micro areas such as development, health, labor, and political economy. Consistent with the credibility revolution, the same shift appears within fields: in econometrics, the share of papers on causal inference more than doubled while the share on econometric theory halved. Despite this reallocation, the discipline has not drifted apart. The distances between research clusters are unchanged since 2000, and the degree of collaboration and cross-pollination between fields has increased markedly. Indeed, I show that the research clusters that grew most are those whose researchers and citations reach into distant fields. An interactive version of the map is publicly available.

Social Capital in the United Kingdom: Evidence from Six Billion Friendships
Tom Harris, Shankar Iyer, Tom Rutter, Drew Johnston, and 9 more authors, Martin Wessel, Guanghua Chi, Patrick Lam, Antonio Silva, Lucy Makinson, Mei-Chen Liou, Yingcan Wang, Qamar Zaman, Michael Bailey
2025
Abstract

We construct a range of measures of social capital (the strength and structure of social networks) for the United Kingdom using data from Facebook covering 20.5 million adults. We make these measures publicly available. Low-socioeconomic-status (SES) individuals have far fewer high-SES friends than high-SES individuals do. Areas with more cross-SES friendships display higher levels of intergenerational economic mobility (higher adult earnings for children growing up in disadvantaged families). Most of the difference in connectedness to high-SES individuals between low- and high-SES individuals is due to differences in friending bias—the tendency of people to befriend others similar to themselves even conditional on the possible friends they are exposed to. Areas with more long ties (friendships between people with no mutual connections) also display substantially higher levels of intergenerational economic mobility, and long ties are especially likely to form outside the typical settings in which individuals make friends. The relationships between economic connectedness, long ties, and intergenerational economic mobility remain strong even after controlling for other area-level characteristics. We also make new estimates of intergenerational economic mobility in the UK publicly available, including the first estimates for areas in Scotland and Northern Ireland.

Task Value Inside the AI Time-Horizon Frontier

Abstract

METR measures the capability of AI agents by the maximum length of a task they can reliably complete. This paper estimates the share of the US wage bill comprising tasks inside that capability frontier, alongside the share representing tasks workers actively delegate to AI. By attaching a wage-bill share and a human duration estimate from the Anthropic Economic Index to each of the 18,796 O*NET work tasks, I calculate the expected success probability implied by METR's fitted logistic curves. Under the latest model METR has measured, tasks carrying 60 per cent of the wage bill are expected to succeed. At the occupational level, no single occupation has 90 per cent of its task value inside the frontier, indicating that AI covers parts of most jobs rather than the entirety of a few. These estimates rely on two structural assumptions: equating AEI's duration estimates for delegated chat tasks with METR's laboratory completion times, and extrapolating success curves from software engineering to the broader economy.
